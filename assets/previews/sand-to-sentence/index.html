0Your bedroom · 23:47

One question, late at night

Your Physics mock is tomorrow. Your notes on scattering make no sense any more. You open a chatbot and type:

Why is the sky blue? Explain it like I'm revising for A-level Physics.

You press send. Words start to appear almost straight away, one small piece at a time.

In that gap, your question will cross a sea cable, wake up billions of tiny switches made from purified sand, and pass through a model built from ideas that came from labs in at least three countries. Let's follow it.

1Your phone, then the chatbot's servers

Your question is chopped into tokens

Before the model can do anything, your sentence is cut up. Not into letters, and not quite into words: into tokens, chunks that the model has a fixed number for.

"Why" might be one token. "Scattering" might be two. A space often sticks to the front of the next word.

What's really happening

Each model has a vocabulary of tokens, often between about 30,000 and a few hundred thousand of them. Common words get a token of their own. Rare words are built from smaller pieces. Every token is really a number, its position in the vocabulary.

For English, a token works out at about four characters, or roughly three-quarters of a word, on average. That's why chatbot limits and prices are counted in tokens, not words.

A model can only read so many tokens at once. That limit is its context window. Your question, the chat so far and the answer it is writing all have to fit inside it.

2The first layer of the model

Each word becomes a point in space

A token number on its own means nothing. Token 9,412 is no closer to "sky" than token 9,413. So the model swaps each token for a long list of numbers called an embedding.

Think of each list as coordinates. Words with similar meanings end up near each other, like towns on a map.

What's really happening

On paper you can draw a map with two coordinates. Large models use thousands. DeepSeek-V3, for example, gives every token a list of 7,168 numbers. Nobody chose those numbers. They were learned during training, by nudging them until the model got better at predicting text.

This huge space of numbers is called latent space. Directions in it can carry meaning. Researchers have found directions that seem to track things like tense, plural versus singular, or even whether a statement is true.

3A data centre, possibly in another country

The trip to a building full of chips

Your question doesn't stay on your phone. It travels as light through fibre-optic cable, perhaps under the sea, to a windowless building the size of several football pitches.

Inside are rows of racks, each packed with chips, humming fans and pipes of cooling liquid. Thousands of other people's questions are arriving at the same moment. Software groups them together so the chips are never idle. That job is called model serving.

What's really happening

Answering your question is called inference: running a model that has already been trained. It uses far less computing than training did, but it happens billions of times a day, so the total adds up.

All that computing turns electricity into heat, and the heat has to be removed. Some data centres use large amounts of water for cooling. This is why AI companies now sign deals for power stations, and why some local communities have pushed back against new data centres.

  • AI is also used to design better batteries and to manage power grids. Should that count in its favour?

4Inside one chip

Thousands of workers, one huge sum

Your question arrives at a GPU, a graphics processing unit. These chips were first made to draw video games, which means working out millions of pixels at once.

That turned out to be exactly what AI needs. Running a language model is mostly one kind of maths, repeated billions of times: multiplying big grids of numbers called matrices.

What's really happening

A CPU, like the one in your laptop, has a handful to a few dozen powerful cores. Each one is fast and flexible and good at jobs that must happen one step after another. A GPU swaps that for thousands of simpler cores that all do the same kind of sum at the same time. NVIDIA's H100 has nearly 17,000 of them, plus special units built just for matrix maths. Google's TPUs and other AI accelerators go further: they are designed for nothing else.

The surprise is that the maths is often not the slowest part. A model is its parameters, billions of stored numbers. To write each new token, the chip has to read every number it uses from memory. So AI chips sit next to stacks of very fast memory called HBM (high-bandwidth memory), and the fight is often about moving numbers quickly enough, not doing sums quickly enough.

5Rewind: a beach, a furnace, a crystal

The chip was once sand

Pause your question for a moment and rewind the chip it's running on. Years ago, it was quartz: silicon dioxide, the main ingredient of sand.

Quartz is heated with carbon in an electric furnace to pull the oxygen away. The silicon is then purified again and again until it is one of the purest materials people make. It's melted, and a single perfect crystal is slowly pulled out of the melt, like lifting a stick out of honey. That crystal is sliced into thin, mirror-polished discs called wafers.

What's really happening

Silicon is a semiconductor. On its own it barely conducts, but adding a tiny amount of another element changes that. A few boron atoms give it gaps for electrons, called holes (p-type). A few phosphorus atoms give it spare electrons (n-type). This is called doping.

Put p-type and n-type regions side by side and you can build a transistor: a switch with no moving parts, turned on and off by a voltage. One switch can store or pass a 1 or a 0. Billions of them, wired together with copper, make a processor. In today's smallest transistors, the insulating layer that controls each switch is made with hafnium oxide, because silicon dioxide made that thin would leak.

About 28%of the Earth's crust by mass is silicon, more than any element except oxygen. Getting it pure enough for chips is the hard part: semiconductor-grade silicon is about 99.9999999% pure (nine nines), and often purer.

6Veldhoven, the Netherlands · Hsinchu, Taiwan

Printing with light no one can see

How do you draw billions of switches onto a wafer? With light. The pattern is projected onto a light-sensitive coating, a bit like an old film camera, and then etched in, layer after layer.

The trouble is that you can't draw lines much finer than the wavelength of the light you draw with. So the industry went to an extreme: extreme ultraviolet light, with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres. To make it, a laser hits tiny falling droplets of molten tin, about 50,000 times a second, heating each one into a glowing plasma.

What's really happening

Only one company in the world makes these EUV machines: ASML, based in Veldhoven in the Netherlands. Each one is the size of a bus and costs well over a hundred million euros. The most advanced chips are then mostly made by one company, TSMC, in Taiwan.

That makes the chip supply chain one of the most concentrated in the world economy. Governments have noticed. Since 2022 the US has restricted the sale of the most advanced AI chips, and the tools to make them, to China. In 2023 China began requiring export licences for gallium and germanium, two elements used in chipmaking and electronics, of which it produces most of the world's supply.

"Process nodes" such as "3 nanometre" sound like measurements, but they no longer measure any part of the transistor. They're closer to brand names for each new generation.

7Back inside the model

The model guesses the next word, again and again

Back in the data centre, your embeddings flow up through the model's layers, often about 60 to over 100 of them. In each layer, every token looks back at all the tokens before it and asks: which of these matter to me?

"Blue" pays attention to "sky". "Explain" pays attention to "A-level Physics". This step is called attention, and it is the heart of the transformer, the design Google researchers published in 2017.

What's really happening

At the top of the model, out comes a list of probabilities for every token in the vocabulary: how likely each one is to come next. One token is chosen, added to the end, and the whole thing runs again for the next token. That's why answers appear word by word.

The model isn't looking up a stored answer. It's producing a likely-sounding continuation, based on patterns learned in training. Usually that's right. Sometimes it's confidently wrong. A setting called temperature controls how adventurous the choice is.

8AI labs in the US and China

Every model is built from other models' ideas

The model answering you didn't come from nowhere. Before it could answer anything, it was trained: shown trillions of tokens of text for months, on thousands of GPUs, adjusting its parameters a tiny bit after every guess.

And the design it was trained with is a patchwork. News stories talk about an "AI race" between the US and China. Look closely at how the models are built, though, and you'll see ideas passing back and forth across the Pacific, often within months.

What's really happening

This works because many labs publish their research, and many release open-weight models: the trained parameters themselves, free to download, study and build on. Meta's LLaMA, DeepSeek, Qwen (from Alibaba) and Kimi (from Moonshot AI) are all open-weight.

Chips shape the ideas, too. US export rules meant Chinese labs had less access to the most powerful chips, which pushed them to squeeze more out of the hardware they had. DeepSeek reported training its V3 model on 2,048 export-compliant NVIDIA H800 GPUs, a small number by frontier standards. That success pushed the whole industry towards efficiency tricks like the ones below.

9Your bedroom · 23:47 and a few seconds

The answer comes home, and you check it

Token by token, the answer travels back under the sea and onto your screen. It talks about sunlight being scattered by molecules in the air, and how shorter wavelengths scatter far more than longer ones. That's Rayleigh scattering.

Then it says the effect depends on the wavelength to the power of two. You frown. Your notes say four. Your notes are right: the scattering goes as 1/λ⁴. The model has just hallucinated, a fluent, confident sentence that is wrong.

You correct it, write the right version in your notes, and go to bed. You were the human in the loop.

What's really happening

Not every model needs a data centre. Small language models, and big ones shrunk by quantisation and distillation, can run on a phone or laptop. This is called edge AI. It keeps your data on your own device, works offline, and needs no distant power station, but the models are less capable.

Wherever the model runs, the same rule holds. It predicts likely text. It does not check facts the way you can. That's what AI Awareness Day 2027 means by Keep Humans in the Loop.

For teachers

Using this in a lesson

The story works on its own for independent reading or homework. For a 60-minute lesson, try this:

0–5Hook. Ask: "What physically happens between pressing send and the first word appearing?" Collect guesses on the board.
5–20Read in pairs. Chapters 0 to 4. Each pair answers the quick checks, then tries the race and the memory calculator.
20–30Calculate. Using the calculator: can an 8-billion-parameter model run on a phone with 8 GB of memory? What about a 70-billion one? Which precision makes it fit?
30–45Read and debate. Chapters 5 to 8. Split the class into the three debates (energy, chip supply, the AI race) and have each group report its strongest argument on each side.
45–55Hallucination hunt. Chapter 9. Students ask a chatbot something they've studied in their own subject and check the answer against their notes. Did they catch anything?
55–60Exit ticket. "Name one element from each periodic table that your question depended on, and say why."
