0Your bedroom · 23:47

One question, late at night

Key points
  • One question will pass through a model, a chip and a global supply chain.
  • You will collect tiles from two periodic tables: chemistry and AI.

1Explain

Your Physics mock is tomorrow. Your notes on scattering make no sense any more. You open a chatbot and type:

Why is the sky blue? Explain it like I'm revising for A-level Physics.

You press send. Words start to appear almost straight away, one small piece at a time.

In that gap, your question may cross a sea cable, wake up billions of tiny switches made from purified sand, and pass through a model built from ideas that came from labs in at least three countries. Let's follow it.

What's really happening

What you just typed is a prompt: the instruction or input you give an AI model to get a response. Yours has the two parts a good prompt usually has: an instruction (“Why is the sky blue?”) and context (“like I'm revising for A-level Physics”), which tells the model how to pitch its answer.

The chatbot runs on a large language model, or LLM: an AI model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word (more precisely, the next token, which Chapter 1 explains). It doesn't look up a stored answer. It writes a new one, one token at a time, which is why the words appear piece by piece.

Inside the data centre, your prompt goes through four stages. Each has its own chapter, so here’s just the outline:

  • It’s cut into tokens: chunks of text, each with a number (Chapter 1).
  • Each token becomes an embedding: a long list of numbers that captures its meaning. Words with similar meanings get similar numbers, so “sky” ends up close to “blue”, and far from “banana”. This is how a model handles meaning at all, because it can only do maths on numbers (Chapter 2).
  • Layers of attention let each token weigh up the words around it, so “blue” in “why is the sky blue” is read differently from “feeling blue” (Chapter 7).
  • The model scores every possible next token, picks one, and repeats: the demo above.

Go further: writing a better prompt

A clearer prompt makes a good answer more likely. Five things to try, each built on your question:

  • Instruction: say exactly what you want. “Explain why the sky is blue.”
  • Context: who it’s for, and what you already know. “I’m revising for A-level Physics and I know what wavelength means.”
  • An example: show the style you want, and the model copies the pattern. This is called few-shot prompting. “Answer like this: Red sunsets happen because…”
  • Format: ask for the shape of the answer. “Three bullet points, then one exam-style sentence.”
  • Step by step: ask it to work through the reasoning first. “Work through it step by step, then give the answer.” Part 3 shows why this helps, and when it doesn’t.

Then check the answer. A better prompt makes a good answer more likely, not certain: Chapter 9 shows a model getting this very question wrong.

1Your phone, then the chatbot's servers

Your question is chopped into tokens

Key points
  • Text is cut into tokens: words or pieces of words, each with a number.
  • A token is about three-quarters of an English word.
  • The context window is the most tokens a model can take in at once.

1Explain

Before the model can do anything, your sentence is cut up. Not into letters, and not quite into words: into tokens, chunks that the model has a fixed number for.

"Why" might be one token. "Scattering" might be two. A space often sticks to the front of the next word.

What's really happening

Each model has a vocabulary of tokens, often between about 30,000 and a few hundred thousand of them. Common words get a token of their own. Rare words are built from smaller pieces. Every token is really a number, its position in the vocabulary.

For English, a token works out at about four characters, or roughly three-quarters of a word, on average. That's why chatbot limits and prices are counted in tokens, not words.

A model can only read so many tokens at once. That limit is its context window. Your question, the chat so far and the answer it is writing all have to fit inside it.

2The first layer of the model

Each word becomes a point in space

Key points
  • Each token becomes an embedding: a long list of numbers that captures its meaning.
  • Similar meanings sit close together in this latent space.
  • The numbers are learned in training, not written by people.

1Explain

A token number on its own means nothing. Token 9,412 is no closer to "sky" than token 9,413. So the model swaps each token for a long list of numbers called an embedding.

Think of each list as coordinates. Words with similar meanings end up near each other, like towns on a map.

What's really happening

On paper you can draw a map with two coordinates. Large models use thousands. DeepSeek-V3, for example, gives every token a list of 7,168 numbers. Nobody chose those numbers. They were learned during training: the months-long process, before anyone uses the model, of showing it huge amounts of text and nudging all its numbers after every guess so that its next guess is a little better. Chapter 8 comes back to it.

This huge space of numbers is called latent space. Directions in it can carry meaning. Researchers have found directions that seem to track things like tense, plural versus singular, or even whether a statement is true.

Go further: Maths and Computer Science

How does a model measure whether two embeddings "mean" similar things? A common way is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between the two vectors.

cos θ = (a · b) ÷ (|a| |b|)

It's 1 when the vectors point the same way, 0 when they're at right angles, and −1 when they point in opposite directions. Try it with a = (3, 4) and b = (4, 3). You should get 24 ÷ 25 = 0.96: very similar.

3A data centre, possibly in another country

The trip to a building full of chips

Key points
  • Your question is answered in a data centre, not on your phone.
  • Answering is called inference, and it happens billions of times a day.
  • Data centres used about 1.5% of the world’s electricity in 2024.

1Explain

Your question doesn't stay on your phone. It travels as light through fibre-optic cable, perhaps under the sea, to a windowless building the size of several football pitches.

Inside are rows of racks, each packed with chips, humming fans and pipes of cooling liquid. Thousands of other people's questions are arriving at the same moment. Software groups them together so the chips are never idle. That job is called model serving.

What's really happening

Answering your question is called inference: running a model that has already been trained. It uses far less computing than training did, but it happens billions of times a day, so the total adds up.

Using AI like this is called cloud AI: the model runs on powerful computers in a data centre, and you reach it over the internet rather than running it on your own device. Most chatbots work this way. Chapter 9 looks at the alternative.

All that computing turns electricity into heat, and the heat has to be removed. Some data centres use large amounts of water for cooling (the Responsible lesson How AI uses our drinking water looks at how much, and where). This is why AI companies now sign deals for power stations, and why some local communities have pushed back against new data centres.

4Inside one chip

Thousands of workers, one huge sum

Key points
  • GPUs do thousands of simple sums at once, which suits AI’s matrix maths.
  • A model is billions of parameters, read from memory for every token.
  • Memory speed, not arithmetic, is often the bottleneck.

1Explain

Your question arrives at a GPU, a graphics processing unit. These chips were first made to draw video games, which means working out millions of pixels at once.

That turned out to be exactly what AI needs. Running a language model is mostly one kind of maths, repeated billions of times: multiplying big grids of numbers called matrices.

What's really happening

A CPU, like the one in your laptop, has a handful to a few dozen powerful cores. Each one is fast and flexible and good at jobs that must happen one step after another. A GPU swaps that for thousands of simpler cores that all do the same kind of sum at the same time. NVIDIA's H100 has nearly 17,000 of them, plus special units built just for matrix maths. Google's TPUs and other AI accelerators go further: they are designed for nothing else.

The surprise is that the maths is often not the slowest part. A model is its parameters, billions of stored numbers. To write each new token, the chip has to read every number it uses from memory. So AI chips sit next to stacks of very fast memory called HBM (high-bandwidth memory), and the fight is often about moving numbers quickly enough, not doing sums quickly enough.

One way to win that fight is quantisation: storing each parameter with fewer digits, a bit like rounding 3.14159 to 3.1. Using 4 bits instead of 16 needs a quarter of the memory, so the model is smaller, faster and cheaper to run, but it can become slightly less accurate. You can try it in the memory calculator in “Go further”, below.

Bytes = parameters × bits ÷ 8. A real system needs extra memory on top, for example to remember the conversation so far. An H100 can read roughly 3.35 terabytes per second from its memory.

If every new token means reading every parameter once, memory speed sets a ceiling on how fast one person's answer can be written:

tokens per second ≤ memory bandwidth ÷ bytes read per token

An 8-billion-parameter model at 16 bits is 16 GB. On an H100 at about 3,350 GB/s, that's at most 3,350 ÷ 16 ≈ 209 tokens a second for one user.

Now the clever part. If 32 people's questions are handled together, the parameters are read once and used 32 times. That's called batching, and it's why one GPU can serve many people at once. What does this tell you about the GPU's arithmetic units while it serves just one person?

5Rewind: a beach, a furnace, a crystal

The chip was once sand

Key points
  • Chips are made from silicon purified from quartz sand.
  • Adding boron or phosphorus (doping) makes transistors possible.
  • The newest AI chips hold over 200 billion transistors.

1Explain

Pause your question for a moment and rewind the chip it's running on. Years ago, it was quartz: silicon dioxide, the main ingredient of sand.

Quartz is heated with carbon in an electric furnace to pull the oxygen away. The silicon is then purified again and again until it is one of the purest materials people make. It's melted, and a single perfect crystal is slowly pulled out of the melt, like lifting a stick out of honey. That crystal is sliced into thin, mirror-polished discs called wafers.

What's really happening

Silicon is a semiconductor. On its own it barely conducts, but adding a tiny amount of another element changes that. A few boron atoms give it gaps for electrons, called holes (p-type). A few phosphorus atoms give it spare electrons (n-type). This is called doping.

Put p-type and n-type regions side by side and you can build a transistor: a switch with no moving parts, turned on and off by a voltage. One switch can store or pass a 1 or a 0. Billions of them, wired together with copper, make a processor. In today's smallest transistors, the insulating layer that controls each switch is made with hafnium oxide, because silicon dioxide made that thin would leak.

6Veldhoven, the Netherlands · Hsinchu, Taiwan

Printing with light no one can see

Key points
  • Chips are printed with extreme ultraviolet light, 13.5 nanometres long.
  • Only ASML makes EUV machines, and TSMC makes most of the most advanced chips.
  • That concentration makes chips a question of global politics.

1Explain

How do you draw billions of switches onto a wafer? With light. The pattern is projected onto a light-sensitive coating, a bit like an old film camera, and then etched in, layer after layer.

The trouble is that you can't draw lines much finer than the wavelength of the light you draw with. So the industry went to an extreme: extreme ultraviolet light, with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres. To make it, a laser hits tiny falling droplets of molten tin, about 50,000 times a second, heating each one into a glowing plasma.

What's really happening

Only one company in the world makes these EUV machines: ASML, based in Veldhoven in the Netherlands. Each one is the size of a bus and costs well over a hundred million euros. The most advanced chips are then mostly made by one company, TSMC, in Taiwan.

That makes the chip supply chain one of the most concentrated in the world economy. Governments have noticed. Since 2022 the US has restricted the sale of the most advanced AI chips, and the tools to make them, to China. In 2023 China began requiring export licences for gallium and germanium, two elements used in chipmaking and electronics, of which it produces most of the world's supply.

This is why governments now talk about AI sovereignty: a country's ability to build, run and control its own AI, data and computing power, rather than relying on other countries or companies. With the chip supply chain this concentrated, almost no country has it completely.

"Process nodes" such as "3 nanometre" sound like measurements, but they no longer measure any part of the transistor. They're closer to brand names for each new generation.

7Back inside the model

The model guesses the next word, again and again

Key points
  • Attention lets each token weigh up the tokens before it.
  • The model scores every possible next token, picks one, and repeats.
  • Temperature controls how adventurous each pick is.

1Explain

Back in the data centre, your embeddings flow up through the model's layers, often about 60 to over 100 of them. In each layer, every token looks back at all the tokens before it and asks: which of these matter to me?

"Blue" pays attention to "sky". "Explain" pays attention to "A-level Physics". This step is called attention, and it is the heart of the transformer, the design Google researchers published in 2017.

What's really happening

Attention is how each token gathers context. It gives every earlier token a score for how relevant it is, then blends in information from the ones that score highest. In “Why is the sky blue?”, “sky” scores high for “blue”, so after attention the numbers for “blue” mean the colour of the sky, not a mood.

A transformer is the design behind today’s large language models, introduced by Google researchers in 2017: dozens of layers of attention stacked on top of each other. Each layer lets every word weigh up how it relates to the words around it, and builds a richer sense of the whole passage.

At the top of the model, out comes a list of probabilities for every token in the vocabulary: how likely each one is to come next. One token is chosen, added to the end, and the whole thing runs again for the next token. That's why answers appear word by word.

To avoid redoing work for every new token, the model keeps notes on all the tokens so far: for each layer, the "keys" and "values" that attention uses. This store is called the KV cache. It grows with every token of the conversation, which is one reason long chats use so much chip memory, and why the memory calculator in chapter 4 says a real system needs extra memory on top.

The model isn't looking up a stored answer. It's producing a likely-sounding continuation, based on patterns learned in training. Usually that's right. Sometimes it's confidently wrong. A setting called temperature controls how adventurous the choice is.

Everything in this chapter, token after token, is inference (you met the word in Chapter 3): using a trained model to produce output from a new input. Nothing inside the model changes while it answers. Its parameters were fixed when training finished.

Go further: Maths

The model's last layer gives every token a score z. The scores become probabilities with the softmax function, divided by the temperature T:

pi = ezi/T ÷ Σj ezj/T

Every probability is positive and they add up to 1. As T gets small, the top score's share heads towards 1. As T gets large, all the probabilities head towards being equal. Use the slider to check, then explain why from the formula.

8AI labs in the US and China

Every model is built from other models' ideas

Key points
  • Models are trained on trillions of tokens over months.
  • Key ideas pass between US and Chinese labs in both directions.
  • Published research and open-weight models make that possible.

1Explain

The model answering you didn't come from nowhere. Before it could answer anything, it was trained: shown trillions of tokens of text for months, on thousands of GPUs, adjusting its parameters a tiny bit after every guess.

And the design it was trained with is a patchwork. News stories talk about an "AI race" between the US and China. Look closely at how the models are built, though, and you'll see ideas passing back and forth across the Pacific, often within months.

What's really happening

This works because many labs publish their research, and many release open-weight models: the trained parameters themselves, free to download, study and build on. Meta's LLaMA, DeepSeek, Qwen (from Alibaba) and Kimi (from Moonshot AI) are all open-weight.

Chips shape the ideas, too. US export rules meant Chinese labs had less access to the most powerful chips, which pushed them to squeeze more out of the hardware they had. DeepSeek reported training its V3 model on 2,048 export-compliant NVIDIA H800 GPUs, a small number by frontier standards. That success pushed the whole industry towards efficiency tricks like the ones below.

One trick that spread this way is distillation: training a smaller “student” model to mimic the behaviour of a larger “teacher” model, by learning from the teacher's answers. The student keeps much of the teacher's skill at a fraction of the size and cost. It's also why labs worry about rivals quietly distilling their models.

9Your bedroom · 23:47 and a few seconds

The answer comes home, and you check it

Key points
  • Models predict likely text. They don’t check facts.
  • Small models can run on your own device: edge AI.
  • You are the human in the loop. Check what matters.

1Explain

Token by token, the answer travels back under the sea and onto your screen. It talks about sunlight being scattered by molecules in the air, and how shorter wavelengths scatter far more than longer ones. That's Rayleigh scattering.

Then it says the effect depends on the wavelength to the power of two. You frown. Your notes say four. Your notes are right: the scattering goes as 1/λ⁴. The model has just hallucinated, a fluent, confident sentence that is wrong.

You correct it, write the right version in your notes, and go to bed. You were the human in the loop.

What's really happening

Not every model needs a data centre. A small language model is a compact model designed to run quickly and cheaply, often directly on a device: a few billion parameters instead of hundreds of billions. Small models, and big ones shrunk by quantisation and distillation, can run on a phone or laptop. This is called edge AI. It keeps your data on your own device, works offline, and needs no distant power station, but the models are less capable.

Wherever the model runs, the same rule holds. It predicts likely text. It does not check facts the way you can. That's what AI Awareness Day 2027 means by Keep Humans in the Loop.

When you checked the formula against your notes, you were the human in the loop: a person who reviews, approves or corrects what an AI produces before it takes effect. Here it only went into your notes. When AI decisions affect money, health or someone's job, that check matters far more.
