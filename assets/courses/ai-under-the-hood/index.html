How these courses work

Learn it by watching it, then doing it

A story, not a lecture

Every chapter starts with a moment in one student's life, then explains what is really happening underneath.

Animated flows

Watch work move through a system step by step: a question becoming tokens, a document being retrieved, an agent deciding what to do.

Your turn

Put the steps in order yourself and the diagram builds as you go. Then try the calculators, simulators and quick checks.

Two periodic tables

Collect real chemical elements and terms from the AI Literacy Periodic Table as you read, and combine them into compounds.

Nodes challenges

Build answers, don’t just pick them. Six in each course, from the Nodes method: keep two confusable ideas apart, join an answer link by link, judge three students’ answers, find the step where an answer broke, and try a new case with the help hidden.

Debates that matter

Who should control the chips? Who is to blame when an agent goes wrong? Each course ends with questions worth arguing about.

The teaching model

Every chapter, the same four steps

1 · Explain, with examples

A real moment from a student’s day, then “What’s really happening”, with every term defined in context and a summary of definitions. Research: concrete examples before abstract ideas.

2 · Predict, see, say why

Guess what will happen, then watch the animated flow show whether you were right, and why. Research: guessing first (pretesting), and dual coding (words with pictures).

3 · Check

A quick check, then a harder Nodes challenge: sort, order or judge, so you build an answer instead of just picking one. Research: retrieval practice and the generation effect.

4 · Sum it up

The chapter’s key points, then one question per element to earn its tile and open the next chapter. Research: retrieval practice.

Parts 2 and 3 open with three questions from the part before. The courses work best when a teacher runs them in a lesson: the Education Endowment Foundation’s guidance on digital technology finds that how teachers use what students show, and how students act on feedback, matter more than the tool itself.
