0The week after exams

One agent, too many jobs

Key points
  • One agent given a huge job loses track: its context fills up and errors pile up.
  • Engineers use design patterns: reusable shapes for building AI systems.

1Explain

Exams are over (Parts 1 and 2 got you through them). The sixth form votes to end the year with a charity quiz night on Friday 2 July, and somehow you've ended up in charge.

You give your agent from Part 2 the whole job: Organise the leavers' charity quiz night. Book the hall, write eight rounds of questions, make a poster, sell tickets and answer everyone's messages.

By Wednesday it's a mess. It has written the history round twice, forgotten a parent's question from Monday, and one of its answers is wrong. One agent, given everything at once, keeps losing track.

What's really happening

Remember what an agent is. At its heart is a large language model: an AI model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. An AI agent wraps that model in a loop, so it can pursue a goal by deciding on and taking actions, not just answering questions.

A single agent struggles with a big, messy job for three reasons:

  • Its context window fills up with everything it has read and done, so early details get pushed out or overlooked.
  • Every step is another chance for a mistake, and mistakes carry forward into later steps.
  • One set of instructions has to cover very different jobs, from writing jokes to handling money.

Engineers fix this the way people organise any big job: with a few reusable shapes, called design patterns. Most of them are things you already do in group projects. Split the work up. Check it. Take a vote. Ask whoever is in charge.

1Workflows

Draw the steps yourself

Key points
  • In a workflow, people fix the steps. In an agent, the model chooses them.
  • Chaining, routing and parallelisation are the main workflow patterns.
  • Start with the simplest thing that works.

1Explain

Your Computing teacher, Ms Okafor, takes one look and asks a simple question: "You already know the steps. Why let the AI decide them?"

So you write a fixed recipe. Questions are written first, then checked, then laid out on answer sheets. Every incoming message is sorted before anyone replies. And the eight rounds are written at the same time, by eight separate requests, instead of one after another.

What's really happening

Engineers at Anthropic draw a useful line between two kinds of system. In a workflow, people decide the steps in advance and write them into the code. In an agent, the model decides its own next step. Their advice is to start with the simplest thing that works, and only use an agent when the steps really can't be known in advance.

  • Prompt chaining: the output of one model request becomes the input to the next. A gate between steps checks the work and stops the chain if something fails.
  • Routing: a first request sorts each input into a category, then sends it to a prompt, tool or model built for that category. Easy questions can go to a small, cheap model and hard ones to a bigger one.
  • Parallelisation: independent pieces run at the same time and are merged at the end. You'll meet another kind in chapter 2: running the same task several times and comparing the answers.

Workflows are predictable, cheaper and easier to test. Agents are flexible. Most real systems mix both.

2Reasoning patterns

Check it, vote on it, try again

Key points
  • Reflection, evaluator-optimiser and voting make answers more reliable, at extra cost.
  • Self-checks often miss errors when there is nothing outside to test against.
  • Voting only helps if the model is right more often than wrong.

1Explain

The questions are written. Now the answers have to be right. Nothing ruins a quiz night faster than a wrong answer read out to a hall full of parents.

The puzzle round includes a classic: A bat and a ball cost £1.10 in total. The bat costs £1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

Most people answer 10p straight away. The right answer is 5p. Will the AI get it right?

What's really happening

Several patterns make one model more reliable, at the cost of more requests.

  • Reflection: the model reads its own draft, criticises it and rewrites it. It catches some mistakes, but research shows that models checking themselves, with nothing from outside to go on, often miss their own errors.
  • Evaluator-optimiser: one request writes, a separate one grades the work against clear criteria, and the loop repeats until it passes. It works best when "good" can be tested, like code that must pass its tests. Evals are tests that score an AI’s output against clear criteria, like a mark scheme. Evaluator-optimiser runs an eval inside the loop, every time.
  • Self-consistency: ask the same question several times and keep the most common answer. This is where temperature from Part 1 matters. Near zero, you'd get almost the same answer every time, and there would be nothing to vote on.
  • Plan-and-execute writes out every step before acting. Tree of thoughts explores several lines of reasoning, like branches, and drops the weak ones.

Many of these rely on chain of thought: getting the model to write out its reasoning step by step before it answers. That often helps with maths and logic, though the written steps don't always show how the model really reached its answer.

Worked example

Draft, check, fix

QuestionA bat and a ball cost £1.10 in total. The bat costs £1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?
First draftThe ball costs 10p.
Same model, asked to check its draftIf the ball is 10p, the bat is £1.10, and together they cost £1.20, not £1.10. The draft is wrong.
Revised answer, reasoning shownCall the ball x. The bat is x + £1.00, so 2x + £1.00 = £1.10. That gives 2x = 10p, so the ball costs 5p.

Here the check works because the model tested its answer against the question. Asked only "Are you sure?", models often just repeat themselves, or change a right answer to a wrong one.

Simplified: every wrong answer here is the same wrong answer (10p), like a shared misconception, and each answer is independent of the others. Real answers are not fully independent, so real gains are smaller.

Go further: Maths

The voting simulator uses the binomial distribution. If each answer is right with probability p, the chance that a majority of 5 answers is right is:

P = Σk=35 C(5,k) pk (1−p)5−k

At p = 0.7 that is 0.309 + 0.360 + 0.168 ≈ 0.837: the vote is right about 84% of the time, against 70% for one answer. Now try p = 0.4. What happens, and why?

3Multi-agent patterns

A manager and a team of specialists

Key points
  • A lead agent can hand jobs to specialists and combine their results.
  • Team patterns include supervisor, hand-off, agent-as-tool and debate.
  • Teams cost far more: about 15 times the tokens of a chat in Anthropic’s report.

1Explain

Some problems can't be planned for. On Thursday, the caretaker emails: the hall's projector is broken. Can the picture round still work? Suddenly there's research to do, a round to rewrite and a poster to change.

So you set up a team. A lead agent reads each new problem, decides which jobs it needs, and hands them to specialist agents: a researcher, a question writer and a designer. Each one gets only the instructions and tools for its own job. They work at the same time and report back, and the lead agent puts it all together.

What's really happening

This is the orchestrator-workers or supervisor pattern. It looks like parallelisation from chapter 1, with one big difference: the jobs aren't fixed in advance. The lead agent decides them as it goes. That’s the difference from workflow orchestration in chapter 1: there, people fixed the steps in advance; an orchestrator decides them while the job runs. That's planning: breaking a goal down into ordered steps and deciding what to do next, and here the plan changes whenever a new problem arrives. Anthropic uses this shape for Claude's Research feature, where a lead agent sends several sub-agents to search at once.

  • Hierarchical teams: supervisors of supervisors, like a head teacher, heads of department and teachers.
  • Hand-off: control passes to another agent, which takes over the conversation, like a phone call put through to the right department.
  • Agent-as-tool: the main agent calls another agent like a function and stays in charge. The other agent just returns an answer.
  • Debate: two agents argue for different answers over several rounds, and a judge decides. Each can point out mistakes the other missed.

Teams aren't free. Every agent reads its own instructions and context, which costs tokens, money and electricity. Anthropic reported that its single agents used about 4 times as many tokens as a chat, and its multi-agent systems about 15 times as many. On its own test of hard research questions, the team scored about 90% better than a single agent. A team is worth it only when the job is worth the cost.

4Humans in the loop

Where should the human sit?

Key points
  • Small chances of error add up over long jobs: 0.98⁵⁰ is only about 36%.
  • People asked to approve everything stop checking. That is automation bias.
  • Put people on the risky steps, and keep their list short.

1Explain

You learned in Part 2 to make the agents ask before doing anything that matters. So by Thursday night your phone buzzes every few minutes. Approve? Approve? Approve? Somewhere around the twentieth request, you stop reading and just tap Approve.

Request twenty-three was the deposit for the hall. The agent had misread the price list and paid £350 for the whole weekend, instead of £35 for one evening. The school office spotted it the next morning and got the money back. You were in the loop. You just weren't looking.

What's really happening

Each extra step is another chance for a mistake, and over a long job small chances add up. If each step goes right 98% of the time, the chance that fifty steps in a row all go right is 0.9850, only about 36%.

Checks help: guardrails that filter what goes in and out, an AI evaluator, or a person. But people have a weakness too. Automation bias is our tendency to trust a computer that is usually right, and to stop checking it. Ask someone to approve too many things, and approving becomes a reflex.

How much an AI system can decide and do on its own before a person steps in is its autonomy. Keeping a person involved to review, approve or correct its decisions before they take effect is called human-in-the-loop. The two pull against each other, and the design question is where to set the dial.

So good designs put people where they matter most: before actions that spend money, can't be undone, or speak for someone. Automatic checks handle the rest. Requests for approval are few, and say exactly what will happen.

Calculator

How likely is a long job to go right?

Made-up but reasonable assumptions: the AI checker catches half of all mistakes. You catch 9 in 10 at first, but 3 percentage points fewer for every request you've already been asked (never fewer than 1 in 10). One step in five is risky, like spending money. Mistakes are independent.

Go further: Maths

How many steps before a job is more likely to fail than to succeed? Solve pn = 0.5 with logarithms:

n = ln 0.5 ÷ ln p

At p = 0.98, n = −0.693 ÷ −0.0202 ≈ 34. After about 34 steps, the job is more likely to have gone wrong somewhere than not. What does p need to be for 100 steps to have a 90% chance of success?

5Putting it together

Pick the pattern

Key points
  • Start simple, add checks where mistakes cost most, and use teams only when the job needs one.
  • Keep a person on anything that spends money.

1Explain

Friday 2 July. The hall is full, the picture round works on paper, and the bat-and-ball question catches out half the parents. You raise £640 for charity.

Afterwards you leave a note for next year's organisers: Start simple. Add checks where mistakes cost most. Use a team only when the job needs one. Keep a person on anything that spends money, and keep their list short.

What's really happening

You've just run a piece of agentic AI: AI that can plan, use tools and carry out several steps of a job by itself, with a person checking in only now and then. Every system in this course is a mix of the same few patterns. The table sums them up.

For teachers

Using this in a lesson

Works on its own, or as a third lesson after From Sand to Sentence and From Answer to Action.

By the end, students can:
  • explain the difference between a workflow and an agent, and say when each is the better choice;
  • describe how reflection, evaluator-optimiser and self-consistency make answers more reliable, and explain when a vote makes things worse;
  • compare multi-agent patterns (supervisor, hand-off, agent-as-tool, debate) and weigh their benefits against their cost;
  • calculate how the chance of error grows over many steps, and argue where human checks should go.

For one 60-minute lesson (two lessons are suggested below):

0–5Hook. Ask: "Think of a group project that went wrong. Was the problem the plan, the checking or the teamwork?" Sort answers into three columns on the board. They map onto chapters 1, 2 and 3.
5–15Workflows. Chapters 0 and 1. Pairs route the inbox twice, with the "send to a person" switch on and then off. Which messages go wrong, and why?
15–27Checking. Chapter 2. Before reading, everyone answers the bat-and-ball question on a mini whiteboard. Then use the vote: find a setting where voting makes things worse, and explain why. Maths students can check the percentages with the binomial distribution.
27–37Teams. Chapter 3. Run the team and note the final token count. Was a team worth roughly 15 times the cost for this job? What job would be worth it?
37–50The human. Chapter 4. Pairs use the calculator at 10 steps and at 50 steps: which design gives the best result for the fewest approvals? Then the debate.
50–57Sort. Chapter 5 individually, then compare answers.
57–60Exit ticket. "Your school wants an AI to answer parents' emails. Which pattern would you start with, and where would a person check?"

Two lessons? Lesson 1: chapters 0 to 2, with the pattern map, the workflow flow and its puzzle, the inbox router and the voting simulator. Lesson 2: chapters 3 to 5, with the team run, the "where the human sits" flow, the calculator and the debate, then the final quiz. Use the Key terms list as a starter for Lesson 2.
