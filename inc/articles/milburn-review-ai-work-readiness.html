In short:

  • Alan Milburn’s final report on young people and work wants a Ready-for-Work Standard for schools, and AI skills training available to every young person.
  • Most young people already use AI, but few think it will create more opportunities for them. Schools are where that gap can start to close.
  • Keep humans in the loop: data that flags a pupil at risk should start a conversation with a trusted adult, and a new standard shouldn’t become another dashboard for teachers.

On 17 November 2026 Alan Milburn published the final report of his review of young people and work, From a Welfare State to a Working State: A new settlement for the next generation. Nearly one million young people, one in eight, are not in education, employment or training (NEET). The report’s ambition is to bring that rate down to around 5 per cent by 2036.1 The government responded the same day with a promise of a funded college place for every young person who wants one, and said plans for curriculum reform and vocational GCSEs would follow in the coming weeks.2

Much of the report is about welfare, health and employers. This post looks at what it asks of schools, and at what being ready for work means when AI is changing both the first jobs young people apply for and how they apply.

What the review asks of schools

  • From 2028, a Ready-for-Work Standard that assesses each pupil and adds up to a published school ready-for-work rate, including the gaps for pupils on free school meals and with SEND. Ofsted would give it equal weight with existing headline measures, and destination measures would judge schools more on where pupils go and whether they stay there.
  • A statutory key stage 3 check in writing, numeracy and oracy, with schools given discretion over around 10 per cent of curriculum time.
  • A lighter GCSE exam load.
  • Work experience as a statutory duty: employer projects, visits, talks and tasters in key stage 3, and a real workplace placement in key stage 4, spread across the year rather than at exam time.
  • Risk of NEET Indicator (RONI) tools, which use school data to spot pupils at risk of becoming NEET, made mandatory in primary and secondary schools.1

These are recommendations, not policy yet. The government’s first response was about colleges, with curriculum plans to follow in the coming weeks.2

School leaders have responded cautiously. Paul Whiteman of the National Association of Head Teachers (NAHT) said schools “do not need another accountability measure judged by Ofsted”. Leora Cruddas of the Confederation of School Trusts warned against “over-emphasising the role of schools”. Daniel Kebede of the National Education Union (NEU) welcomed fewer GCSE exams and the key stage 3 flexibility, while criticising the extra tests and targets.3 Milburn himself acknowledges that schools “often feel overwhelmed”, and argues the changes are still justified.1 We come back to workload below.

What the report says about AI

The report warns that young people “now face the risk of an AI-led employment meltdown”, but its recommendations are careful. It says it would be wrong to blame AI for the recent rise in young people dropping out of work and learning, and that nobody can know exactly what AI’s impact will be over the next decade. But it warns of “a real risk that young people bear a disproportionate share” if the entry-level tasks through which earlier generations learned how to work disappear faster than new routes are created.1

The first rung is already thinner. Entry-level vacancies have fallen by 49 per cent over the past decade. Recruitment has become more formal, more remote and increasingly automated, and the report notes that a young person can now be rejected for doing badly in a recorded interview, or for not knowing how an automated application system works.1 The report calls this the “rejection economy”: application after application, often with no feedback, and rejections that arrive months later.1 Josh Hillman of the Nuffield Foundation warns that AI-mediated hiring could make it worse.4

The report’s answers include:

  • training in AI skills available to every young person, building on initiatives such as the AI Skills Hub;
  • a small AI Youth Observatory to track where entry-level tasks and jobs are shrinking or changing;
  • employers testing automated recruitment for barriers that have nothing to do with the job, and businesses that receive significant public support showing how they will keep building their entry-level pipeline as technology changes;
  • assessment that keeps supervised exams while finding rigorous ways to test judgement, problem-solving and practical competence.1

The report also listened to young people. In the Youth Voice Census it draws on, 69 per cent had used AI or machine-learning tools, but 55 per cent said technological change made them uncertain about the future, and only 19 per cent believed it would create more opportunities.1 Young people are not avoiding AI. Many just can’t see where they fit in the jobs market it is helping to shape.

A young person can be punctual, literate and numerate and still be filtered out by a system they have never been told exists. The report doesn’t say who should deliver AI skills training or what it should cover, only that it should reach the places at greatest risk of disruption. School is the one place that reaches every young person, so work-readiness has to include knowing how these systems work and how to reach the human behind them.

“This is a welcome review. As teachers, we have many ways to get young people into good tech jobs: mock interviews, hackathons, changes to courses, developing software and creating the qualifications industry needs. But the glaring missing issue is that many young people who do have AI and tech qualifications are still finding it hard to break into the industry. What chance does a young person who is NEET have?”

Mark Martin MBE, UKBT Institute

That is why the employer side of Milburn’s bargain matters as much as the school side. Skills open the door only if there is a first job behind it.

What work-ready means now

The report says every young person should leave school knowing how to find an opportunity, complete an application and approach an interview.1 It doesn’t define “AI skills”, so here is our suggestion, using the five AI Awareness Day strands. Smart, for example, answers the report’s warning that pupils are rejected for not knowing how automated application systems work.

Oracy: the part no tool can do for you

The review backs a statutory key stage 3 check in oracy, alongside writing and numeracy. It is clear this “should be a diagnostic check, not a new high-stakes exam”, there to find pupils who need extra support while there is still time to give it.1 It lands alongside the government’s decision to give oracy equal status with reading and writing in England’s national curriculum. The revised curriculum is due to be finalised in spring 2027 and taught from September 2028, as we set out in oracy and AI Awareness Day.

AI makes this more important, not less. When a tool can draft a CV or a cover letter in seconds, what sets a young person apart is being able to explain their thinking, answer a follow-up question and listen properly to someone else. That is the part of an interview no tool can do for them.

AI itself is a good subject to practise on: current, contested and close to pupils’ lives. Each of our free five-minute lesson starters poses one big question with a UK fact to react to. In November 2026 we began piloting the National Debate Network with primary, secondary and post-16 students, and the youth-led National AI Conversation opens on 1 January 2027.

Keep humans in the loop

Under the review’s proposals, every school would use RONI tools to spot pupils at risk of becoming NEET. These bring together information schools already hold, such as attendance, attainment, care experience, SEND and exclusions.1 A data flag can help a busy school notice a pupil sooner. It can’t build the trust that keeps that pupil coming to school. The AI Awareness Day 2027 theme, Keep Humans in the Loop, suggests three questions for any RONI tool:

  • Does every flag lead to a conversation with a named adult?
  • Do pupils and families know what data is used, and why?
  • Can a teacher who knows the pupil overrule a wrong flag?

The report sets the same rule for the predictive tools it proposes in health services: they “should support professional judgement, not make decisions” on a young person’s behalf. It takes the same view of careers guidance: AI-enabled tools should free up more face-to-face time for the young people who need it most.1 And it applies to employers. If AI does the first sift of applications, a young person should still be able to reach a person and get feedback they can use.

Use the new space, without adding workload

If 10 per cent of the key stage 3 timetable sits outside the national curriculum, many subjects will bid for it. The report’s own examples include debating, enterprise and employer-led projects.1 AI literacy doesn’t need its own slot or a new course: it fits inside careers education, work experience preparation and oracy practice. AI Awareness Day, on Thursday 29 April 2027, asks schools to run just one activity, and our free resources are organised by the five strands.

Leaders are right to worry that a Ready-for-Work Standard could turn into one more thing to measure. Before adding any new metric, it is worth asking:

  1. Does it change what happens for a pupil, or only what gets reported?
  2. Who checks the data, and can a pupil or family question it?
  3. What will teachers stop doing to make time for it?

Try it this week:

  1. Ask a key stage 3 class: “If a computer reads your job application first, what should it look for, and what might it miss?”
  2. Have pupils answer “Tell me about a time you acted on feedback” out loud, then compare their answer with an AI-written one you prepared in advance. Which sounds like a real person, and why?
  3. In a staff meeting, ask how your school would explain a RONI flag to a pupil and their family.

Milburn is right that getting ready for work can’t wait until a young person leaves school. The schools that do this best will teach pupils how AI is changing work, and keep a person beside every pupil the data flags.
